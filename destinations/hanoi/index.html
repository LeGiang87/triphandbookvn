A. Overview

Hanoi is Vietnam's capital, and it feels like it — a thousand years old, a little chaotic, and completely itself. The city has been a capital since 1010, when King Ly Thai To moved his court here and named it Thang Long, "the ascending dragon." Today you can walk from a 1,000-year-old temple to an egg coffee to a water puppet show in a single afternoon, all inside about two square kilometers.

The historic core sits around Hoan Kiem Lake and the Old Quarter, a maze of 36 old guild streets, each once named after the goods sold there. Around it are the French Quarter with its wide boulevards and colonial buildings, Ba Dinh with the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum and the Imperial Citadel, and Tay Ho (West Lake), where the city slows down and breathes.

How many days? Two days covers the core: the Old Quarter and the lake, the Temple of Literature, Hoa Lo Prison, a food crawl and a water puppet show. Three days lets you add West Lake, the Museum of Ethnology and a day trip — Ha Long Bay or Ninh Binh are both an easy day out. Hanoi also works as a base: most visitors to northern Vietnam start and end here.

The best time is October to April. Autumn (October-November) is the sweet spot: dry, cool, golden light, and the city at its most comfortable. December to February is cool and sometimes misty, with Tet (Lunar New Year) bringing festivals and closures. March-April warms up with spring rain. May to September is hot and humid, 30-35°C, with sudden afternoon downpours — still very doable, just plan your walking for the morning and evening.

Hanoi suits almost everyone: first-timers who want to feel Vietnam in one city, food lovers (this is the best eating city in the country), history lovers, couples, families, and backpackers. It suits you less if you hate traffic noise and crowds — the Old Quarter is loud, the motorbikes never really stop, and the sidewalks belong to parked bikes and plastic stools as much as to pedestrians.

B. Getting There

By plane. Noi Bai International Airport (HAN) sits about 30 km north of the Old Quarter, a 40-60 minute drive depending on traffic. From the airport, bus 86 is the cheapest sensible option at about 45,000 VND (~$2) per person, running from the airport to the Old Quarter and ending at Hanoi Railway Station. A GrabCar costs roughly 250,000-350,000 VND (~$10-13) to the Old Quarter, with the fare shown in the app before you confirm; a 7-seat GrabCar runs about 320,000-450,000 VND (~$12-17). A metered taxi from the official queue costs about 300,000-450,000 VND (~$12-17) plus tolls of around 45,000 VND (~$2). Use only Mai Linh or Vinasun from the official rank, insist on the meter, and ignore anyone inside the terminal offering a taxi — the "pretend official" meter scam, with demands of 700,000-900,000 VND, is a real thing at Noi Bai. There is a marked Grab pickup zone outside arrivals.

By train. Hanoi Railway Station is the southern end of the Reunification Express line to Ho Chi Minh City, and the starting point for trains to Ninh Binh (about 2 hours 15 minutes, 100,000-230,000 VND), Hue, Da Nang and Lao Cai (the overnight train to Sapa). Book on dsvn.vn or at the station.

By bus. Long-distance buses arrive at My Dinh, Giap Bat, Gia Lam and Nuoc Ngam stations. From Ho Chi Minh City, the bus takes more than 30 hours — fly instead. From Ha Long, Ninh Binh, Hai Phong and other northern cities, buses and limousine vans take 2-3.5 hours.

From Ho Chi Minh City. Fly: about 2 hours, with many daily flights. This is the only sensible option for most travelers.

C. Getting Around

The Old Quarter and the area around Hoan Kiem Lake are best on foot — the historic core is only about two square kilometers, and walking is how you find the good stuff. That said, Hanoi traffic is the real deal: cross the street slowly and predictably, never stop or run, and let the motorbikes flow around you like water around a stone. It feels terrifying the first time and normal by the third.

Grab is the default way to get around the wider city. A short city hop under 5 km costs about 30,000-70,000 VND (~$1-3) by GrabCar and 15,000-25,000 VND (~$0.60-1) by GrabBike. Grab shows the fare before you book, and surge pricing kicks in during rush hour (roughly 07:00-09:00 and 17:00-19:00) and in the rain. Xanh SM (electric taxis, listed as "Green SM: Electric Mobility" on the App Store) is the other big app, often slightly cheaper off-peak.

Metered taxis: use only Mai Linh (green) or Taxi Group (yellow) and check the company name on the door before boarding — fake Mai Linh taxis with tampered meters operate around tourist areas. The flag fall is about 12,000-15,000 VND (~$0.50-0.60), then roughly 12,000-17,000 VND (~$0.50-0.65) per km. Published hotlines: Mai Linh 1800 6868 and Taxi Group 1800 5454 (found in one 2026 guide — see Unverified). Always confirm the meter is running before the car moves.

Cyclos (three-wheeled bicycle taxis) are a fun, slow way to see the Old Quarter, but agree the price in full before you get on — 100,000-150,000 VND (~$4-6) for a short ride is a fair reference, and the classic scam is a quoted price that multiplies at the end.

City buses cost 7,000-9,000 VND (~$0.30) per ride and run from about 04:30 to 22:30. The metro has two elevated lines running as of 2026. Motorbike rental runs about 100,000-300,000 VND (~$4-12) per day, but Hanoi is the hardest place in Vietnam to ride — if you have never ridden in Asian city traffic, do not learn here. You need a valid International Driving Permit plus your home licence, and a helmet is mandatory.

D. Things to Do

1. The Old Quarter and Hoan Kiem Lake — start here

The Old Quarter is the maze of 36 guild streets north and west of the lake. Each street was once named for its trade — Hang Bac (silver), Hang Ma (paper offerings), Hang Gai (silk) — and the names stuck even as the goods changed. It is free to wander, and the best way to see it is on foot, with no plan at all: a bowl of pho here, a coffee there, a temple doorway you were not looking for.

Hoan Kiem Lake (Sword Lake) is the city's center of gravity. The lakeside is free and open 24 hours; early morning is when locals do tai chi and exercise around the water, and the whole place changes character at the weekend. From 19:00 Friday to midnight Sunday the streets around the lake close to traffic and become a pedestrian zone with night-market stalls along Hang Dao and Hang Duong. Cross the red Huc Bridge to Ngoc Son Temple on its little island — the temple charges a small entry fee, most recently 50,000 VND (~$2), though older guides still quote 30,000 VND, so check at the gate.

2. Temple of Literature — Vietnam's first university

Built in 1070 and dedicated to Confucius, the Temple of Literature (Van Mieu - Quoc Tu Giam) became Vietnam's first national university. Five courtyards lead past the Khue Van Pavilion — the building on the back of the 100,000 VND banknote — to stone turtles carrying the names of scholars. Go early in the morning before the tour groups and the graduation photo shoots take over. A separate night tour runs on selected evenings (typically Thursday to Sunday, 19:00-22:00) for 199,000 VND (~$8) — book ahead.

3. Hoa Lo Prison — the heaviest hour in Hanoi

Built by the French in the 1890s to hold Vietnamese revolutionaries, and later used to hold American pilots, who nicknamed it the "Hanoi Hilton" with the blackest humor. The audio guide is worth it, and the exhibits are honest about both eras. The night tour "Dem thieng lieng" (Sacred Night), Friday to Sunday evenings, runs about 399,000-499,000 VND (~$15-19) and sells out in peak season — book in advance.

4. Imperial Citadel of Thang Long — a thousand years on one site

A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2010, the citadel at 19C Hoang Dieu has been the political heart of Vietnam for over a thousand years, with archaeological layers from the Ly, Tran and Le dynasties under the later Nguyen-era gates. The night tour "Decoding the Thang Long Imperial Citadel" runs Friday and Saturday evenings at 19:00 for 300,000 VND (~$12) and lasts about 90 minutes. Note: restoration of Kinh Thien Palace began on 29 September 2026 and runs until 2030; the official site still listed normal hours in early November 2026.

5. Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum complex — mornings only

The mausoleum holds Ho Chi Minh's preserved body, and visits run in the morning only, roughly 07:30-10:30 from April to October and 08:00-11:00 from November to March, on Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Saturday and Sunday. It is closed on Mondays and Fridays, and it closes for two to three months of annual maintenance each year — in 2026 that was 4 September to 2 November, reopening on 3 November. Dress respectfully, no shorts or sleeveless tops, no cameras inside, and expect a queue. The complex also includes Ho Chi Minh's stilt house, the One Pillar Pagoda and the Presidential Palace grounds.

6. Train Street — see it the right way

The famous Train Street is the narrow residential corridor where the north-south railway runs inches from the front doors of people's homes, between Phung Hung and Le Duan streets. The street is officially closed to free-roaming tourists: barricades and guards block the main entrances, and you cannot simply wander in alone. The legal way is to go with a cafe owner — walk to the barricade and a cafe worker will invite you in to sit at their cafe. Trains pass mostly in the evening on weekdays (after 19:00) and throughout the day on weekends. Do not stand on the tracks for photos, and do not argue with the guards — the closures exist because people have been hurt.

7. West Lake and Tran Quoc Pagoda — where Hanoi slows down

West Lake (Ho Tay) is Hanoi's largest lake, ringed by cafes, temples and a cycling path. Tran Quoc Pagoda, nearly 1,500 years old and the oldest Buddhist temple in the city, sits on its own little islet off Thanh Nien Road, reached by a short causeway with water on both sides. Go near sunset, when the red stupa lights up against the water.

8. Water puppet show — a thousand-year-old art form

Water puppetry (mua roi nuoc) comes from the rice paddies of the Red River Delta, where puppeteers stood waist-deep in water behind a screen. At the Thang Long Water Puppet Theatre beside Hoan Kiem Lake, the show lasts about 50 minutes, with live traditional music and no dialogue — easy to enjoy in any language. Book the evening show a day or two ahead, especially on Saturdays.

E. Food & Drink

Hanoi is the best eating city in Vietnam, and the best food is often the cheapest: a bowl of pho on a plastic stool at 06:30, bun cha at lunch, egg coffee in the afternoon. A typical street meal runs 40,000-80,000 VND (~$2-3). Here are the dishes and the places to try them.

Pho — the beef (or chicken) noodle soup that defines Hanoi. Pho Thin, with its stir-fried beef, and Pho Gia Truyen, with its rich clear broth, are the two classic names. A bowl costs about 40,000-60,000 VND (~$2).

Bun cha — grilled pork patties and slices in a sweet-sour broth with vermicelli noodles and herbs, eaten at lunch. Bun Cha Huong Lien, the "Obama bun cha" from the 2016 visit with Anthony Bourdain, is the most famous; Bun Cha Dac Kim on Hang Manh is the other classic. Expect 50,000-80,000 VND (~$2-3).

Egg coffee (ca phe trung) — Hanoi's invention from 1946, when milk was scarce and egg yolk was whipped with sugar and condensed milk over strong robusta coffee. Cafe Giang, where it was invented, is the original. About 25,000-30,000 VND (~$1).

Cha ca — turmeric-marinated fish sizzled at your table with dill, peanuts and noodles. Cha Ca La Vong has served only this one dish since 1871, at about 170,000 VND (~$7) per person, drinks not included.

Banh mi — the Vietnamese baguette sandwich. Banh Mi 25 on Hang Ca is the reliable Old Quarter pick, about 25,000-40,000 VND (~$1-2).

Bun dau mam tom — vermicelli, tofu and fermented shrimp paste, for the adventurous. If shrimp paste is not your thing, you have been warned — and it is still worth trying once.

Vegetarian food: Hanoi has plenty of vegetarian restaurants, especially around the Old Quarter and Tay Ho, and most street stalls will make a vegetarian pho or bun if you ask — say "Toi an chay" (I eat vegetarian) and "khong nuoc mam" (no fish sauce), because fish sauce hides in many "vegetable" dishes. Allergies: peanuts and shrimp paste appear everywhere; if you have a severe allergy, carry an allergy card written in Vietnamese and bring your own medicine. Drinking water: bottled water everywhere; ice at established restaurants is factory ice and generally safe.

F. Where to Stay

Stay in or near the Old Quarter if it is your first time: you can walk to the lake, the food and the sights, and Grab covers the rest. The French Quarter (around the Opera House and Trang Tien) is quieter and more polished, with big hotels. Tay Ho (West Lake) suits longer stays and people who want space and cafes, but you will take a Grab everywhere.

Budget travelers will find dorm beds in the Old Quarter for roughly 150,000-300,000 VND (~$6-12) per night and private rooms in small hotels for 400,000-800,000 VND (~$15-31). Mid-range boutique hotels in the Old Quarter run about 1,000,000-2,500,000 VND (~$38-96) per night. High-end hotels run from about 3,500,000 VND (~$135) per night and up. Prices move a lot with weekends, holidays and Tet — check again when you book. Compare stays on the official homepages of Agoda and Booking.com (plain links, no affiliate code).

G. Sample Itineraries

1 day — the essentials

Morning: pho at Pho Gia Truyen, then Hoan Kiem Lake and Ngoc Son Temple as the city wakes up. Walk the Old Quarter guild streets, stop for egg coffee at Cafe Giang. Lunch: bun cha at Bun Cha Huong Lien. Afternoon: Temple of Literature, then Hoa Lo Prison. Evening: water puppet show at 18:30, then dinner and a walk around the lake.

2 days — the classic Hanoi trip

Day 1: as the 1-day plan above. Day 2: Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum in the morning (go early, check the day — closed Mondays and Fridays), then the Imperial Citadel of Thang Long. Lunch: cha ca at Cha Ca La Vong. Afternoon: Train Street with a cafe, then West Lake and Tran Quoc Pagoda at sunset. Evening: the weekend walking street around the lake if it is Friday to Sunday, or a food crawl through the Old Quarter night market.

3 days — slow Hanoi plus a day trip

Days 1-2: as above, at a slower pace, with the Vietnam Museum of Ethnology and the Vietnamese Women's Museum added. Day 3: a day trip — Ha Long Bay by cruise (leave Hanoi about 08:00, back about 20:00) or Ninh Binh (Trang An boat in the morning, Hang Mua at sunset).

H. Money & Budget

Prices checked November 2026.

Daily budgets per person (not including international flights):

  • Backpacker: 700,000-1,200,000 VND (~$27-46) per day. Dorm bed, street food (pho, bun cha, banh mi), walking and GrabBike, one or two ticketed sights.
  • Mid-range: 1,500,000-2,500,000 VND (~$58-96) per day. Boutique hotel in the Old Quarter, sit-down restaurants, GrabCar, a water puppet show and two or three sights.
  • Comfortable: from 3,000,000 VND (~$115) per day. High-end hotel, private car and driver, guided food tour, fine dining.

Cash is useful for street food, markets and small stalls, but cards are widely accepted in hotels, larger restaurants and shops. ATMs are everywhere in the Old Quarter and the French Quarter. Currency exchange: use banks or gold shops on Ha Trung Street in the Old Quarter — the rates are usually better than at the airport or hotel, and avoid street money changers. The reference rate used in this guide is 1 USD ≈ 26,000 VND. Tipping is not expected in Vietnam, but rounding up the bill or leaving 20,000-50,000 VND for good service is appreciated.

Hanoi's own scams — and how to handle them

Airport taxi touts: drivers inside the terminal offer "metered" rides that turn out to have tampered meters or a multiplied fare. Handle it: walk past them to the official taxi rank or the Grab pickup zone, and use only Mai Linh or Vinasun with the meter running.

The shoe-shine and fruit-vendor photo trap: a vendor puts fruit on your shoulder or shines your shoe without asking, then demands payment — sometimes aggressively. Handle it: say no before they touch you or your shoes, and do not accept a photo with their goods. If you want the photo, agree the price first (about 20,000-30,000 VND).

Cyclo price multiplying: the ride is quoted at one price and charged at several times more at the end. Handle it: agree the full price in writing or by message before you get on, and pay exactly that.

Motorbike bag-snatching: it happens, mostly at night on quiet streets. Handle it: wear your bag across your body on the building side, not the road side, and do not hold your phone out over the road while walking.

Crossing the street: walk slowly and steadily, keep going, and never run or stop suddenly. The riders will calculate your path and flow around you — it works, but only if you are predictable.

Seasonal hazards

Typhoon season in the north runs from late May to early November, peaking from July to October — but typhoons mostly hit the coast and Ha Long Bay, not Hanoi itself; the city gets the rain and wind, and flights can be delayed. Summer (May–August) is hot and humid, with midday temperatures of 35–38°C and heatwaves that have passed 40°C in April and May. Winter (December–February) is cool and damp, with drizzle and fog rather than heavy rain — pack a layer. See the full picture on our Seasonal Hazards & Natural Dangers page.

J. Local Tips

Dress for temples and the mausoleum: covered shoulders and below the knee at the Temple of Literature, Tran Quoc Pagoda and the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum. Take your hat off inside shrine halls.

Bargaining: it applies at Dong Xuan Market, the night market and souvenir stalls — start at about half the asking price and meet in the middle, with a smile. Do not bargain over street food, restaurants with a priced menu, or Grab.

Weather by season: October-November is the best — dry and cool. December-February is cool and misty; bring a light jacket. May-September is hot and humid with afternoon rain; carry a light rain jacket and plan walking for the morning and evening. Tet (Lunar New Year, usually late January or February) is festive but many shops and restaurants close for several days — check dates before you book.

What to pack: comfortable walking shoes (you will walk a lot on uneven sidewalks), a light rain jacket, sunscreen, insect repellent for West Lake evenings, and a small bag you can wear across your body.

Eating like a local: sit on the plastic stool, point at what someone else is eating if you do not know the name, and eat quickly — street food stalls turn tables fast, and lingering is not the custom. Chopsticks and a spoon are the norm; a fork is available if you ask.

K. Tours & Tickets

Hanoi is easy to explore on your own, but a few things are worth booking ahead: the water puppet show (especially Saturday evenings), the Hoa Lo Prison and Imperial Citadel night tours, and a street food tour on your first evening — a good guide will take you to stalls you would never find alone. Day trips to Ha Long Bay and Ninh Binh are best booked as a package with transport included, since the logistics from Hanoi are the hard part.

N. Local Specialties & Souvenirs

Hanoi is one of the best cities in Vietnam for edible souvenirs — most of them are made right here and travel well in a suitcase. Buy food gifts in the Old Quarter, where the specialist streets have sold the same things for generations, and check the expiry date before you pack them.

1. O mai (preserved fruit)

O mai is Hanoi's classic gift snack: plums, apricots, kumquats and star fruit preserved with sugar, salt, chilli and ginger, so each piece is sweet, sour, salty and spicy all at once. It keeps for weeks, weighs almost nothing, and goes perfectly with tea — which is exactly why Hanoians have given it as a gift for generations. Hang Duong Street in the Old Quarter is the o mai street; the shops there have sold it for decades.

2. Banh com (green rice cake)

Banh com is a soft, pale-green sticky rice cake filled with sweetened mung bean paste, made from com — the young green rice flakes of Lang Vong village. It is the taste of Hanoi in autumn, and it is the cake Hanoians traditionally give at weddings and engagements. Eat it within a day or two; it does not keep like o mai does. Hang Than Street is the banh com street, and Nguyen Ninh is the best-known shop there.

3. Van Phuc silk

Van Phuc has woven silk for over 1,000 years, and its silk is famously soft, light and slightly iridescent. A silk scarf is the most packable souvenir here, and fabric by the metre is a good buy if you plan to have something tailored in Hoi An later. See Section O for the village itself.

O. Craft Villages

1. Bat Trang Pottery Village

Bat Trang, on the southern bank of the Red River about 13-14 km southeast of the Old Quarter, has made ceramics for over 700 years. Hundreds of family workshops and kilns line the village lanes, and almost every shop sells pieces made a few dozen metres away — rice bowls, teapots, vases and hand-painted decorative ware. You can watch potters at the wheel, wander the lanes, and try throwing clay yourself at a family workshop or at the Bat Trang Ceramic Museum. It is an easy half-day trip: 20-30 minutes by car or taxi from central Hanoi.

Bat Trang Pottery Village attraction

2. Van Phuc Silk Village

Van Phuc is a living silk village, not a museum: more than a thousand households are involved in silk, and the main street is a row of family shops selling fabric, scarves, ao dai and clothing woven on the premises. The umbrella-shaded street just inside the village gate is the photo spot. Come to watch the looms, feel the difference between silk grades, and buy directly from the makers. The Place block, address and prices are in Section N above.

Real vs market silk: Van Phuc silk is soft, breathable and slightly uneven — tiny irregularities are a good sign of hand weaving. Perfectly uniform, very shiny fabric at a very low price is almost certainly synthetic, and most of it comes from factories, not the village looms.

P. Experiences With Locals

1. Cooking class at the Hanoi Cooking Centre

The Hanoi Cooking Centre is a purpose-built cooking school, cafe and shop on the edge of the Old Quarter, near Truc Bach Lake. A typical half-day class starts with a guided walk around a local market — your teacher shows you how Hanoians choose herbs, fish sauce and vegetables — then you cook several Vietnamese dishes hands-on and sit down to eat what you made, with recipes to take home. Classes run Monday to Saturday in small groups. Tell them in advance about allergies or vegetarian needs; they are used to adapting the menu.
