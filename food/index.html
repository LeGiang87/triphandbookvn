Food Guide: What to Eat in Vietnam

Must-try dishes by region, taken from the Food & Drink sections of the destination guides — with the restaurants that serve them. Prices checked November 2026.

Food safety: choose places where you can see the kitchen, ingredients are covered, local customers are eating, and prices are listed on a menu. This site only marks a restaurant as meeting hygiene standards when it passes specific, checkable criteria like these — never on a feeling.

Northern Vietnam

Hanoi

Hanoi is the best eating city in Vietnam, and the best food is often the cheapest: a bowl of pho on a plastic stool at 06:30, bun cha at lunch, egg coffee in the afternoon. A typical street meal runs 40,000-80,000 VND (~$2-3). Here are the dishes and the places to try them.

Pho — the beef (or chicken) noodle soup that defines Hanoi. Pho Thin, with its stir-fried beef, and Pho Gia Truyen, with its rich clear broth, are the two classic names. A bowl costs about 40,000-60,000 VND (~$2).

Bun cha — grilled pork patties and slices in a sweet-sour broth with vermicelli noodles and herbs, eaten at lunch. Bun Cha Huong Lien, the "Obama bun cha" from the 2016 visit with Anthony Bourdain, is the most famous; Bun Cha Dac Kim on Hang Manh is the other classic. Expect 50,000-80,000 VND (~$2-3).

Egg coffee (ca phe trung) — Hanoi's invention from 1946, when milk was scarce and egg yolk was whipped with sugar and condensed milk over strong robusta coffee. Cafe Giang, where it was invented, is the original. About 25,000-30,000 VND (~$1).

Cha ca — turmeric-marinated fish sizzled at your table with dill, peanuts and noodles. Cha Ca La Vong has served only this one dish since 1871, at about 170,000 VND (~$7) per person, drinks not included.

Banh mi — the Vietnamese baguette sandwich. Banh Mi 25 on Hang Ca is the reliable Old Quarter pick, about 25,000-40,000 VND (~$1-2).

Bun dau mam tom — vermicelli, tofu and fermented shrimp paste, for the adventurous. If shrimp paste is not your thing, you have been warned — and it is still worth trying once.

Vegetarian food: Hanoi has plenty of vegetarian restaurants, especially around the Old Quarter and Tay Ho, and most street stalls will make a vegetarian pho or bun if you ask — say "Toi an chay" (I eat vegetarian) and "khong nuoc mam" (no fish sauce), because fish sauce hides in many "vegetable" dishes. Allergies: peanuts and shrimp paste appear everywhere; if you have a severe allergy, carry an allergy card written in Vietnamese and bring your own medicine. Drinking water: bottled water everywhere; ice at established restaurants is factory ice and generally safe.

Ha Long

Ha Long is a seafood city, and the food is the best reason to spend an evening on land. The local specialties are cha muc (grilled squid patties — chewy, smoky, and the thing to bring home as a gift), fresh steamed shrimp and crab, grilled scallops, and ca nhong (Ha Long grouper). Seafood is priced by weight at most restaurants, so always ask the price per kilogram before you order — a "market price" surprise at the bill is the classic Ha Long complaint.

Hong Hanh is the most famous seafood name in Ha Long, with several branches; the Bai Chay branch at 50 Ha Long Street is the one most visitors go to. Expect about 200,000-400,000 VND (~$8-15) per person. Linh Dan and Thuy Chung on the Bai Chay strip are solid alternatives. For something cheaper, the night market and the street stalls near Bai Chay Beach sell grilled seafood, noodles and beer for a fraction of restaurant prices.

Vegetarian food: the Bai Chay area has a few vegetarian-friendly restaurants, and most seafood restaurants will cook vegetable dishes if you ask — say "Toi an chay" (I eat vegetarian) and "khong nuoc mam" (no fish sauce). Allergies: shellfish is everywhere here; if you have a severe allergy, carry an allergy card written in Vietnamese and bring your own medicine. Drinking water: bottled water everywhere; ice at established restaurants is factory ice and generally safe.

Ninh Binh

Mountain goat and crispy rice are the pair you have to try. The goats climb the limestone mountains, so the meat is lean and firm. Common preparations are rare goat with lime (tai chanh), grilled, steamed, stir-fried with lemongrass and chili, and goat hotpot. One plate serves two people alongside other dishes.

Sa Pa

Sa Pa food is mountain food: warming, simple, and built for cold evenings. Must-tries are thang co (a traditional Hmong horse-meat stew, an acquired taste), salmon and sturgeon hotpot — the cold mountain water is ideal for fish farming — grilled pork and corn on the cob from street stalls, and wild vegetables stir-fried with garlic. The town also has good Vietnamese coffee and a growing cafe scene for slow foggy mornings.

Vegetarian travellers will manage fine: most restaurants do tofu, mushrooms and vegetable hotpot, but say clearly "I eat vegetarian, no meat, no fish sauce" (see section L), because fish sauce hides in many vegetable dishes. Peanuts appear in sauces and salads — flag allergies clearly.

Central Vietnam

Phong Nha

Phong Nha food is simple, fresh and cheap: river fish, grilled pork, morning glory, and rice. The town has a surprising number of good Western-friendly cafes and restaurants run by local families and long-stay travellers — pizza, burgers and proper coffee sit next to Vietnamese staples. Eat at your homestay at least once: family dinners here are generous and a good way to meet other travellers.

Vegetarian options are easy — tofu, mushrooms, vegetable stir-fries and vegetarian hotpot are on most menus. Say "I eat vegetarian, no meat, no fish sauce" (section L). Peanuts appear in sauces and salads, so flag allergies clearly. Drink bottled water; ice in larger restaurants is factory-made and generally safe.

Hue

Hue's food is the most refined in Vietnam — the old imperial court demanded small, beautiful, complex dishes, and the city still cooks that way. Bun bo Hue (spicy beef noodle soup with lemongrass) is the dish the city is famous for. Com hen (clam rice) is the cheap, punchy local lunch. And the city's banh beo (tiny steamed rice cakes) and banh khoai (crispy pancakes) are the snacks to graze on between sights.

Da Nang

Da Nang's food is Central Vietnamese: bold, spicy and cheap. Mi Quang — turmeric noodles with pork, shrimp and herbs in a little broth — is the dish to try first. Bun cha ca (fish-cake noodle soup) is the breakfast of choice, and the beach road is lined with seafood restaurants where you pick your catch from the tanks.

Hoi An

Hoi An has three dishes you will not find done the same way anywhere else: cao lau (thick noodles with pork and greens, made with water from a specific local well), banh mi (the town's version is famous worldwide), and com ga (chicken rice). Eat them in the Old Town, then walk it off along the river.

Southern Vietnam

Nha Trang

Nha Trang's food is all about the sea. The signatures are bun cha ca (fish-cake noodle soup), bun sua (jellyfish noodle soup — the jellyfish is crunchy, not slimy), nem nuong Ninh Hoa (grilled pork rolls wrapped in rice paper with herbs and crispy fried spring rolls), and bo nuong Lac Canh (spicy grilled beef cooked on a small charcoal stove at your table). Seafood restaurants along the northern end of Tran Phu and around the old fishing harbor grill the morning's catch to order.

Da Lat

Da Lat food is built for cool weather: hotpots, grilled meats, hot soy milk and sweet potatoes roasted over charcoal at the night market. Must-tries are banh trang nuong (grilled rice paper, Da Lat's answer to pizza), banh can (mini savoury pancakes), avocado ice cream, artichoke tea, and strawberries — Da Lat grows most of Vietnam's strawberries, and the fresh ones in season are wonderful.

Vegetarian travellers will do well: the city has several vegetarian restaurants (the Zen monastery area is a good bet), and most restaurants do tofu and vegetable hotpot. Say "I eat vegetarian, no meat, no fish sauce" (section L). Peanuts appear in sauces — flag allergies clearly. The tap water is not for drinking; use bottled water.

Ho Chi Minh City

Saigon is one of the great eating cities of the world, and you do not need a big budget to eat brilliantly. The rules are simple: eat where it is busy, eat what the place is famous for, and carry small notes — many of the best spots are cash-only.

Pho is the obvious start, and Saigon's version is sweeter and more herb-heavy than Hanoi's. Com tam (broken rice with grilled pork chop, shredded pork skin, and a fried egg) is the city's soul food, eaten at any hour. Banh mi needs no introduction — the best ones are stuffed to bursting and cost less than a dollar. In the evening, look for hu tieu Nam Vang (Cambodian-style pork noodle soup) and banh xeo (crispy stuffed pancakes).

Vegetarian and allergies: Vegetarian restaurants are easy to find — search for "chay" (vegetarian) on Google Maps, and note that many Buddhist vegetarian places close on the 1st and 15th of the lunar month when they are busiest, not closed. Peanuts, shrimp paste, and fish sauce are everywhere: if you have a serious allergy, carry a Vietnamese allergy card and show it before ordering, because "no peanuts" does not always survive translation in a busy street stall.

Coffee: Saigon runs on ca phe sua da (iced coffee with condensed milk), around 20,000–35,000 VND (~$0.80–$1.35) at street cafes. The Cafe Apartments at 14 Huynh Thuc Khang, overlooking Nguyen Hue Walking Street, are worth the climb for the view alone.

Can Tho & Mekong Delta

Delta food is sweet, fresh and generous: river fish, prawns, tropical fruit and coconut in everything. Must-tries are banh xeo (crispy stuffed pancakes), banh cong (fried mung bean cakes), grilled snakehead fish, sour fish soup (canh chua), hu tieu (noodle soup, best eaten from a boat at the floating market), and fresh fruit — mangosteen, rambutan, pomelo and durian in season. Coconut water is sold everywhere for about 15,000–25,000 VND (~$1).

Vegetarian options are easy: the Delta grows everything, and most restaurants do tofu, mushrooms and vegetable hotpot. Say "I eat vegetarian, no meat, no fish sauce" (section L). Peanuts and shrimp paste appear in many dishes — flag allergies clearly. Drink bottled water; ice in larger restaurants is factory-made and generally safe.

Phu Quoc

Phu Quoc's food is fishing-village cooking: grilled seafood, fish sauce (the island's nuoc mam is famous across Vietnam), and goi ca trich — raw herring salad with shredded coconut, herbs, and rice paper, the dish to try if you try one. Bun quay (a self-mixed noodle soup you stir yourself at the table) is the local breakfast. Seafood restaurants line Tran Hung Dao in Duong Dong and the night market; for the freshest catch, eat at the floating restaurants and beach shacks in Ham Ninh fishing village on the east coast.

Vegetarian and allergies: Vegetarian options are thinner here than on the mainland — the night market has grilled corn, fruit, and vegetarian noodles, and most restaurants will make a vegetable hotpot if you ask. Shellfish and fish sauce are in almost everything, so show a written allergy card if your allergy is serious. Phu Quoc's fish sauce is famously strong; even the dipping sauce at a seafood restaurant will contain it.

Vegetarian & allergies

Vegetarian dishes are available in most tourist areas — say “Tôi ăn chay” (I eat vegetarian) and “không nước mắm” (no fish sauce), because fish sauce is in many “vegetable” dishes. Peanuts and dried seafood appear in many dishes; if you have a severe allergy, carry an allergy card written in Vietnamese and bring your own medicine, because small restaurants rarely list allergen ingredients.

Drink bottled water or filtered water at your hotel. Ice at large restaurants is usually factory ice, safer than loose block ice sold on the street.
